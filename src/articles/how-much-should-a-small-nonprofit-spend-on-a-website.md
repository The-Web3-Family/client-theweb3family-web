*From The Friday Letter, my weekly email for nonprofit founders.*

This week I spent a lot of time on one question: is $3,000 a month too much? I’m still not fully sure. But working through it taught me a lot about how a small nonprofit should think about paying for a website, and I want to share that with you.

## Why I first set the price high

When I started, I set one price: $3,000 a month. I picked it on purpose. I only wanted to work with people who were serious, because a service only works when both sides show up.

Here is what I learned. When someone pays for help, they follow up. They ask questions. They make sure they get their money’s worth. That back and forth is what turns an idea into something real.

My clients love the rhythm. Every Friday they get something finished. They look at it, they get new ideas, and the next Friday they get the next piece.

## The free work problem

I also do a lot of free work. I make videos, write plans, and build pages for people who ask for help. I do it at the same quality as my paid work.

My mentor kept telling me, “You need to give out paid work, not free work.” I didn’t want to hear it at first. Then I noticed a pattern. People who got free work often never followed up. Some took what I made and later paid someone else full price for the same result.

So I’m letting that go. Going forward, I’ll offer about a week of free work so people can see how I work. After that, the help comes through a plan.

## What I offer now

I turned one price into three, so more founders can start:

- **Family Package, $500 a month.** Text and call me anytime, and get a finished piece every Friday.
- **Family Package with Extra Support, $1,500 a month.** Everything above, plus one reserved hour on my calendar each week.
- **Family Package with Extra Extra Support, $3,000 a month.** Everything above, with two reserved hours a week.

Every plan includes websites, donation pages, decks, brand books, and videos. Everything lives in a Google Drive your organization owns. Every plan is month to month.

This same week, I set up three YouTube channels for clients, and next I’m heading back to websites. That’s the kind of work a monthly plan covers: whatever the week needs.

## Questions to ask before you pay anyone

Whoever you hire, these questions will protect your organization and your budget:

- **Who owns the work?** Your website, your files, and your accounts should belong to your nonprofit, under your own email.
- **What do I get, and when?** A clear delivery rhythm beats a vague promise. Ask what you’ll have in your hands each week.
- **Can I stop anytime?** Month to month lets you pause when donations are slow and pick back up when they return.
- **What happens after launch?** A website needs care: new events, new donation drives, new stories. Ask who handles that, and what it costs.
- **Will I talk to the person doing the work?** With a small studio, you often talk to the builder directly, which saves time and mistakes.

## The real answer

The right amount is whatever lets you keep showing up, week after week, without straining your budget. A $500 plan that you keep for a year will do more for your nonprofit than a big one-time project that sits untouched.
